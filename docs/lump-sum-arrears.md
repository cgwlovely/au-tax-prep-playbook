# Back pay and lump sum payments in arrears

*English · [中文](zh/lump-sum-arrears.md)*

An employer that underpaid you in an earlier year and later remediates it creates a payment that behaves differently from ordinary salary. It is reported differently, it can attract a tax offset, and — this surprises people — it usually *reduces* the year's refund.

## 1. Recognising it

The payment arrives as salary, through payroll, often years after the work. Signs:

- the payslip job title says something like "remediation" rather than the original role;
- the payslip carries a line named **Lump Sum E** rather than ordinary hours;
- there is a separate **interest** line alongside it;
- withholding on the payment is a very small percentage.

The employer's income statement reports the Lump Sum E amount separately from ordinary salary.

## 2. What the lodgment form asks

Reporting the Lump Sum E amount is not enough. The form also asks **which income year or years the arrears accrued in, and how much belongs to each**, and it will not accept the return until that is supplied. The entries must sum to the reported Lump Sum E figure.

Only the employer can state this authoritatively. Ask for a year-by-year breakdown, quoting your employee or student ID and the date of their remediation letter.

## 3. Establishing the accrual year when the employer is slow

Two independent lines of evidence usually settle it.

### Historical payslips

Old payslips carry a **year-to-date** figure. Where the remediated work spanned two financial years, compare the YTD for the relevant employment record in each year:

```text
share of arrears likely attributable to a year
  ≈ that year's original earnings for the remediated role
    ÷ total original earnings across all remediated years
```

A payslip dated in late June carries essentially the full-year YTD, which makes it a good proxy for that year's total. Keep these payslips — they are the basis for a judgement you may have to explain.

### The interest component

Where the employer pays interest on the arrears, the ratio of interest to principal reverse-engineers the elapsed period:

```text
implied annual rate = (1 + interest ÷ principal) ^ (1 ÷ years) - 1
```

Try candidate periods. A plausible rate (roughly 4–7% for this kind of remediation) identifies the period; an implausible one (double digits) rules it out. This corroborates the taxpayer's recollection rather than replacing it.

## 4. The offset, and why it often does not apply

The lump sum payment in arrears **tax offset** exists to stop arrears being taxed at today's higher marginal rate when they were earned in a lower-income year. It is subject to a threshold test:

```text
eligible arrears must be 10% or more of
  (taxable income for the year of receipt − the arrears amount)
```

A modest back payment against a full-time salary fails this test easily. Where it fails, the offset is nil and **the accrual year you report changes nothing in the assessment** — the form still demands it, but the figure drives no calculation.

Say this plainly in the handover. It changes how much effort chasing the exact split is worth, without making an inaccurate entry acceptable.

## 5. The unwelcome arithmetic

Employers withhold on remediation payments at a rate that assumes the payment is the person's only income. Someone whose other income already sits in a high bracket therefore has far too little withheld:

```text
additional tax = arrears × current marginal rate (including Medicare)
             less the small amount actually withheld
```

The back pay reduces the refund. That is not an error — it is the consequence of money arriving years late, at a rate the employer could not know. The offset is the designed remedy, and where the threshold test fails there is no remedy. Interest paid by the employer is partial compensation.

## 6. Interest on arrears is not bank interest

Where the employer pays interest through payroll, it is included in the salary figure on the income statement. **Do not also declare it as investment interest.** It will not appear in any bank's reporting, and declaring it twice overstates income.

Whether that characterisation is correct is the employer's call, reflected in what they reported. Report as the income statement shows, and note the composition in the working papers.

## 7. What to keep

- the remediation letter and any year-by-year breakdown;
- every payslip for the remediation payments (they separate Lump Sum E from interest);
- historical payslips for the original work, which establish the accrual years;
- a short note recording the basis for the year you reported, and what would cause you to revise it.

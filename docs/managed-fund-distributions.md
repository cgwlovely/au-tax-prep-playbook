# ETF and managed fund distributions

*English · [中文](zh/managed-fund-distributions.md)*

## 1. No trades does not mean no income

The most common failure here is reasoning from the brokerage account's trade history: nothing bought, nothing sold, therefore nothing to report. An ETF held all year pays distributions whether or not you touch it. Quarterly and semi-annual payers are both common.

Check the **cash movements**, not the trades. A rising cash balance in a brokerage account with no sales is distribution income sitting there.

Pull the full financial year from the broker's transaction history and read the deposit lines. They give the payment date, the per-unit rate and the units held — enough to establish the cash received exactly.

## 2. These are not dividends

An Australian-domiciled ETF is usually a trust, and most are **attribution managed investment trusts (AMITs)**. Their distributions are trust distributions, not company dividends.

The practical test is the form itself: the dividends section offers only unfranked amount, franked amount, franking credits and TFN amounts withheld. There is **nowhere to put foreign income or a foreign income tax offset** — which is what an international equity ETF's distribution mostly consists of. If the income has nowhere to go, you are in the wrong section.

Use the managed fund and trust distributions section instead. Do not tick the separate "foreign income" category either: the foreign component of a trust distribution flows through the trust section, and ticking both invites double reporting.

## 3. What the statement must give you

The annual tax statement (an **AMMA statement** for an AMIT) breaks the distribution into components that map one-to-one onto lodgment fields:

| Statement line | Goes to |
| --- | --- |
| Non-primary production income | the non-primary production income field |
| Franked distributions / franking credits | the franked distribution and franking credit fields |
| Other deductions | the deductions-against-distribution field |
| Net capital gain / total current year capital gains | the capital gains fields, split by method |
| Assessable foreign source income | the assessable foreign income field |
| Foreign income tax offset | the foreign income tax offset field |
| Other income | the other income field |
| **AMIT cost base net amount (increase or decrease)** | **no field — archive it** |

Two things follow.

**The assessable amount is not the cash.** Foreign income is reported gross of foreign tax withheld, and the offset is claimed separately. Gross distribution = cash received + the offset. Reporting the cash figure alone understates income while overstating tax.

**The cost base adjustment never appears on the return.** It adjusts what you paid, for the capital gain calculated whenever you eventually sell. Lose these statements and that calculation becomes unreconstructable years later. Archive one per year, permanently.

## 4. The timing trap

Funds have three months after year end to finalise their attribution, and the annual investment income report that feeds pre-fill is due at the end of October — **the same day as the deadline for self-preparers**. Pre-filled distribution data is therefore routinely unavailable when you need it.

Plan around it:

- ask the broker or registry for the statement rather than waiting for pre-fill;
- a brokerage platform's statements section may hold only account and trading statements, not the tax statement — in a custodial model the fund's statement goes to the custodian, who passes it on separately;
- check email: brokers often send an advance notice explaining when the statement will arrive, which is itself useful evidence of what kind of income you have.

## 5. The fund manager's published components as a fallback

Fund managers publish estimated **per-unit tax components** for the year on the fund's product page. These are not a substitute for your own statement — they are the fund's full-year figures, and a holding bought mid-year receives a different mix — but they are useful twice:

**As a cross-check.** When your statement arrives, compare the component shape against the published percentages. A large divergence means something is wrong with one of them.

**As a documented fallback.** If the statement genuinely cannot be obtained before the deadline, applying the manager's published percentages to your actual cash received is a reasoned position with a citable source, which is far better than omitting the income or inventing a split. Record the source and the date, lodge, and amend when the statement arrives.

Note what the published table settles even before your statement arrives: whether the fund distributed any franked income at all, whether there were capital gains, and whether there is a foreign income tax offset to claim.

## 6. One detail that is easy to miss

Where a distribution contains both discounted capital gains and a foreign income tax offset, part of the offset may relate to the capital gains and has to be apportioned differently. The statement discloses this separately when it applies. A published per-unit summary generally does not split it — so treat an estimate as provisional on exactly this point.
